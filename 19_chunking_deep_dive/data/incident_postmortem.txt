Postmortem: the March checkout outage (INC-20481)

On the morning of 14 March our customers could not create new deployments for almost two hours. This document describes what happened, why it happened and what we are changing. It is written without blame. The goal is to understand the system, not to find a person at fault.

The first alert fired at 08:12 UTC. The deployment queue depth had grown from its normal value of about 20 jobs to more than 4,000 jobs within ten minutes. The on-call engineer, Mira, acknowledged the page at 08:15 and opened a SEV2 incident. At 08:31 she upgraded it to SEV1 because the status page was already receiving reports from large customers. The incident commander role was handed to Tomas at 08:40, and he kept it until the end. A first mitigation, scaling the workers from 40 to 120 instances at 08:52, did not help at all, which told us that the workers were not the bottleneck.

Between 09:00 and 09:45 the team looked in the wrong places. We suspected the database, because its CPU was at 85 percent, but a replica promotion at 09:20 changed nothing. We suspected a bad release, and we rolled back the previous day's build at 09:35, again without effect. Looking back, both guesses were reasonable given the graphs we had, and the lack of a single dashboard that shows queue depth next to lock wait time cost us about 40 minutes.

The root cause was found at 09:48 by Priya, who noticed that every stuck job was waiting on the same row in the quota table. A migration deployed on 13 March had added a trigger that recalculated the organisation quota after every job state change. Under normal load this was harmless. During a busy Monday morning, thousands of jobs for the same few large organisations updated the same quota rows in parallel, and every update had to wait for a row lock held by the previous one. The workers were healthy, but they were all blocked behind these locks, which is why adding more of them only added more waiting transactions.

The fix was simple once the cause was known. At 09:58 we disabled the trigger with a feature flag, and the queue started to drain immediately. By 10:20 the queue depth was back to 35 jobs and we closed the incident at 10:30. In total 6,812 deployments were delayed, with a median delay of 74 minutes. No data was lost, and no customer was billed for the failed attempts, because we credited all affected organisations automatically on 16 March.

Customer impact was uneven. Free and Pro customers saw their deployments stay in the queued state. Enterprise customers have a dedicated queue, so they were affected for only about 25 minutes, until the shared lock contention spread to their jobs as well. Our status page was updated four times, but the first update came 19 minutes after the first alert, which is too slow. The target for the first public update is now 10 minutes.

We are making five changes. First, quota recalculation moves out of the database trigger into an asynchronous job that batches updates once per minute. Second, the load test for every migration must now include a scenario with at least 1,000 concurrent updates to the same row. Third, we are building a combined dashboard that puts queue depth, worker utilisation and database lock waits on one screen. Fourth, the incident commander checklist gets an explicit step to check for lock contention before scaling workers. Fifth, the status page runbook will draft the first public message automatically from the alert text.

Several things went well. The page was acknowledged within three minutes, the incident commander handover was clean, and the feature flag allowed us to disable the trigger without a deploy. The customer credits were automatic and needed no support tickets. One thing was lucky: the migration had a flag at all, because a reviewer had asked for it two weeks earlier.

The action items have owners and due dates in our tracker. The dashboard is due on 5 April, the load test requirement was merged on 21 March, and the asynchronous quota job ships in the release of 11 April. We will review progress in the engineering meeting on 18 April.
